Imagine standing outside a house you grew up in, watching it burn. You may even have lit the match yourself. That, I think, is roughly where the modern West finds itself: outside a home we no longer trust, unsure whether to mourn it, and with nowhere obvious to sleep tonight.

Meaning seems to have drained out of modern life. Maybe it eroded because we abandoned any coherent picture of what reality is and what we are for. Or maybe we looked too closely at the depths of our own longings, grew confused and angry at the structure built on top of them, and decided to set it alight. Many people who invested real hope in looking inward and outward—hoping to find the assurances our ancestors searched for—now feel betrayed. That reaction may be justified. But it leaves us standing in the yard while the fire spreads.

Hope is not lost. Something has to be built from the ashes, and I want to argue that the first step is small: communities deliberately oriented toward shared goals and genuine care for one another.

## A Foundation With Many Stones

The West’s foundations were never one thing. They were laid from Greco-Roman thought, Judeo-Christian ethics, and the wisdom of the many cultures absorbed into the Roman world, later enriched by exchange with the Middle East, Africa, and Asia. That mixture shaped the Western way of seeing, and in the decades after World War II it produced something remarkable: a fortress of production and relative peace.

Over the centuries, several turning points in Western philosophy forced a hard look at those foundations. Since the Enlightenment, we have questioned every metaphysical claim—whether God exists, whether anything transcendent exists at all. In doing so we have interrogated not only our Judeo-Christian inheritance but religion as a whole. And the decline of religious authority has, by many measures, coincided with better living conditions.

Here is the paradox. The same technological explosion we celebrate has multiplied our uncertainties. The more science and technology reveal, the deeper we sink into existential ambiguity. The questioning has turned inward, toward our assumptions about reality, our innermost selves, and the societies we live in.

## Down in the Basement

We have gone down, so to speak, into the basement of our own house. What we found there was unsettling: no shared, agreed-upon account of how we know anything, and therefore nothing to justify our actions or even our knowledge. Acting without purpose leaves us unsatisfied. The stories that once sustained us have faltered. Almost without noticing, we have let go of any unifying story about where Western civilization is headed.

Standing in that basement, the foundations looked flimsy and badly made. It felt less like a cellar than a tomb. So we passed judgment on the whole structure with a sharp, acidic skepticism. Yet this house was handed down to us, and it gave us shelter and material prosperity. What was it originally *for*? What larger purpose did it serve?

There is a second question. What have recent upheavals done to us? They echo the despair of the Great Depression and World War II, when people living through rapid progress expected a golden age and could not make sense of the catastrophes that arrived instead. Our moment carries a similar disillusionment—not just with our institutions and ourselves, but with the universe itself. Without a defining story, life for many people feels like a random sequence of events. We have lost faith not only in a metaphysical picture of the world but in our own abilities.

## Many Small Houses

The result is a clash of responses that grind against one another, visible in the widening split between Republicans and Democrats over which way to go. Beneath that discord I see a deep and genuine longing for a vision of reality worth pledging oneself to.

That vision does not need everyone’s agreement. It may be enough for smaller groups—clusters of people who reach real consensus—to take collective action together. Such groups can drift into conflict, sometimes fierce conflict. Still, in our current moment, this kind of fragmentation may be the most workable place to begin.

Going backward will not work. Even if we rolled back technology or social norms, we could not recreate the exact conditions we idealize. And we cannot simply withdraw. Even a life “off the grid” depends on society to survive; pure subsistence farming, cut off from everyone else, is an illusion, because the threads of modernity eventually wind around us anyway. A historical “reset” is a fantasy. Looking to the future for salvation is equally futile.

## The Neglected Present

We need both memory and anticipation, but they have to be balanced by a virtue we rarely think about: the present. This moment—the one we are actually living—deserves far more respect than we give it.

Consider vacations. They were once a break from the daily “musts.” Now most of us treat them as an escape. There is nothing wrong with a getaway, and sometimes wanderlust simply calls. But why do we need to escape at all? If we improved our ordinary lives as fully as our means allow, could they replace the escapism we cherish? If the balance and richness we are looking for were already present in daily life, how different would things be?

That brings me back to the present. Before we can restore faith in anything larger, we first have to recover faith in ourselves—acting firmly on our hope for the future while staying rooted in the life we actually live.

## Building Before Sundown

We are standing at a crossroads. We condemned our house and are watching it burn, slowly. But if we do not build a new one, we will be left exposed in the wilderness.

The good news hidden in this picture is that the new house can be designed on purpose rather than assembled by accident. We get to choose what goes into it. So I’d urge us to take our time while there is still daylight and lay the foundation carefully. Perhaps small communities, each working on a specific problem or question, can combine their successes into answers to larger problems than our stagnant, bloated institutions have managed to solve.

Writing a single grand story for a civilization that has abandoned its ancestral home is hard. And the limits of what we can know mean we will never be fully certain our beliefs are true. Those limits are not a reason to stop building. They are a reason to reflect carefully on what the new house is made of.

> We will never prove our beliefs true with certainty. That shouldn’t stop us from building—it should make us think harder about what we build with.

The tension in this picture doesn’t go away. Every so often we glance at the horizon and wonder whether anything worth trusting can rise before the sun sets and leaves us exposed in the dark forest. But the sun is still up. The time is still ours.
